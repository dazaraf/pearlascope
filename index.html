02 · Network

Hashrate

How much matmul work miners point at Pearl. Difficulty chases price, so this is the market's real vote on miner margins.

Nobody measures hashrate directly; every site estimates it. Ours: hashrate = difficulty × 248 ÷ average block time, averaged over the last 50 blocks (about 2.5 hours), the same window prlscan shows. Over such a short window one fast or slow block moves it about 2%, so expect a jumpy line.

The PEARL Thesis

The best idea in crypto × AI this year, priced like a GPU coin. The whole bet is whether FP8 turns it into Bitcoin for AI before the dilution eats the holders.

Two professors in Jerusalem rebuilt Bitcoin so the mining is the math AI runs on. The idea is real, the code ships every week, and the lab just raised $130M to prove it pays. Today almost none of the mining is useful, gaming cards do 82% of it, and holders eat 81% more supply over the next year. I'm holding through that because the fix has a name (FP8), it's in an open pull request updated this week, and the market is pricing it like it never lands.

What Pearl is

Bitcoin miners burn about as much electricity as a mid-sized country guessing numbers. The guessing is the security. The guesses get thrown away.

Pearl keeps the Bitcoin part and swaps the puzzle. Miners multiply matrices, the operation that eats almost all of AI's compute. The chain mixes fresh random noise from the last block into both matrices, the GPU multiplies, small tiles of the result become lottery tickets for the next block, and the noise peels back off so an AI customer still gets the right answer. The winner ships a zero-knowledge proof, so a laptop can check the block without redoing an H100 job and nobody's prompt is exposed. Under the hood it's btcd: UTXOs, Taproot addresses starting prl1, a 2.1B cap.

Why I care

Every useful-work chain before this one picked between easy to check and useless, or useful and impossible to check. Pearl is the first serious attempt at both, from Omri Weinstein (matrix multiplication complexity, ex-Nvidia, Hebrew University) and Ilan Komargodski (the ZK proofs it runs on, same department). Between them their research covers both halves of the design. I'll admit the local pride: the most-talked-about AI coin of the year came out of a math department in Jerusalem.

The problem today: it's mostly a GPU lottery

The chain proves the math was done, not that anyone needed it. So the cheapest way to mine is a gaming card multiplying random numbers. Blockworks estimates fewer than 1% of blocks match a real AI model and consumer cards run ~82% of hashrate. Two reasons the real AI servers stay out:

  • Format. Mining only counts INT7 math. Normal models don't run in INT7, so even Pearl's best rebuilt model can only mine on two-thirds of its math.
  • Size. Only big batches fill a tile. Reading a long prompt mines; writing the reply one token at a time mostly doesn't. A summariser earns tickets on 25–40% of its math, a chatbot on ~5%. A dedicated miner: ~100%.

Net: an AI server gives up $1.33–3.15 of paid AI work for every coin it mines. A gaming card pays $0.10–0.23 in power. Nobody sane switches.

The fix: FP8

FP8 is the 8-bit format Nvidia's data-center chips already run AI in. PIP-3 moves mining to it, so standard open models (Llama, DeepSeek, partly the new MoE models) mine without being rebuilt, and the Pearl-GEMM rewrite cuts the slowdown on a serving GPU from 10–15% to 0–3%. That puts the AI server's cost per coin at $0.09–0.43, the same range as a gaming card. That's the whole game. Once serving inference is as cheap a way to mine as running a gaming card, every provider who doesn't mine is leaving money on the table.

The catch: the draft only accepts Nvidia Hopper and Blackwell. RTX 40 and 30 cards and every AMD card get cut off at the fork, and they do most of the mining today. Expect a hashrate air pocket on the day it activates. And it doesn't fix the size problem: chatbots still mine badly.

Who actually flips the switch

Mining lives inside the AI software, so it's whoever runs the software on the GPU that decides, not whoever owns the building. Bitcoin miners turned AI landlords mostly can't: their tenants run the chips. The natural first adopters are serverless providers who run both the GPUs and the software, then GPU marketplaces and neoclouds. That's a thinner pipeline than "every Bitcoin miner switches to Pearl". Which is why the lab raising $130M and selling inference itself on Liquid Inference matters: it's the cleanest test, run by the people best placed to pass it.

The bull loop

A provider that mines can pass the PEARL income on as a lower price. Routers like OpenRouter send traffic to the cheapest provider, so it wins volume. Rivals have to mine to keep up. More mining means more security, more security earns a store-of-value premium, a higher price funds deeper discounts. That's the reflexive version, and it's the one worth $15B+.

The bear case, taken seriously

Jacob from Blockworks ("The $PEARL Bear Case") makes four points. I agree with three of them.

  • It isn't scarce yet. Supply grows ~81% this year against Bitcoin's ~0.8%. Bitcoin's early inflation happened at a few million dollars of market cap when nobody knew how to price it. Pearl is doing it at ~$0.5B. Fair. This is the cost of holding it now.
  • Useful work doesn't create demand for the token. AI customers pay in dollars. A provider's discount is funded by selling PEARL, so more usage means more selling. Fair today. It changes only if compute gets priced or settled in PEARL, which is the lab's stated plan and nowhere near shipped.
  • Cheap rentable GPUs make it attackable. Ethereum Classic, Bitcoin Gold and Vertcoin all got 51%'d by rented hardware. Pearl miners earn ~$1.4M a day against Bitcoin's ~$34M of subsidy. Kryptex alone wins close to half the blocks. Fair, and the concentration is the part I like least.
  • Zcash 2016. ZEC fell 91% in four months while its market cap grew 8x, because supply grew 84x. The comparison is real but the scale isn't: Pearl's next-year growth is 81%, not 84x.

Where I disagree: the bear case prices FP8 at zero. It's an open pull request with merged pieces and a funded team whose own research is the exact thing being built. Zero is the wrong number.

The boring risks

  • Every new number format is a hard fork that drops old hardware. Bitcoin hasn't changed its puzzle in 17 years; Pearl shipped four consensus forks between June and August.
  • Someone builds a Pearl-only chip that out-mines GPUs without serving any AI. Nobody is known to be building one.
  • The top 1% of addresses hold ~71% of supply. The lab hasn't disclosed what it mined in the low-difficulty week.
  • Miners sell fast: about half of pool payouts go straight to one custodian's deposit addresses, and only ~10% is still in miners' wallets a week later.
  • Liquidity sits on small exchanges, and the ticker collides with Perle.

Where I stand

I hold PEARL and I'm not selling here. I'm also not adding until FP8 gets an activation height or a real provider starts mining, because buying ahead of 81% dilution with no date is how you donate to miners. I'd rather be early and small than late and big. If FP8 merges and the AI-server cost per coin really lands at gaming-card levels, this goes from a GPU coin to the only working proof of useful work in crypto, at about 3% of Zcash's market cap.

What I'm watching

The chain, not the timeline. PR #311 merging with an activation height. The share of blocks from real models. The lab's prices on Liquid Inference against the other five providers. A Coinbase listing date. A holdings disclosure. Kryptex dropping under 50%. When the first verified paying customer on mining hardware shows up in Useful Work Watch, the case moves up a tier.

Is it worth buying a card to mine?payback per card

Short answer: not to buy one. The cards that pay back fastest are RTX 40-series, and the FP8 fork as drafted cuts them off, so you're betting the fork stays more than a year away. The RTX 5090 survives FP8 but doesn't pay back at today's price. Renting always loses: an H100 rents for ~$39 a day and mines ~$14. If you already own a gaming card, mining is free money until the fork. Data-center cards (H100 and up) are left out of the chart: at $26k+ they never pay back from mining alone.
